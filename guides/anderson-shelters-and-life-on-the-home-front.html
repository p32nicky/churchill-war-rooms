Anderson Shelters and Life on the Home Front

Backyard shelters and daily survival in wartime Britain.

As the threat of aerial bombing loomed over Britain during World War II, the government and citizens alike had to adapt to a new reality of daily survival. One of the most iconic symbols of this era is the Anderson shelter, a small, corrugated iron structure that provided a safe haven for families in their backyards.

Introduction to the Anderson Shelter

The Anderson shelter was designed by John Anderson, the Lord Privy Seal, in 1938, and was named after him. It was a simple, yet effective design, made from six corrugated iron panels that were bolted together to form a small, underground shelter. The shelter was designed to be buried in the ground, with a layer of soil on top to provide additional protection from bomb blasts.

Daily Life in Wartime Britain

Daily life in wartime Britain was marked by rationing, blackouts, and the constant threat of air raids. People had to be vigilant and prepared at all times, with gas masks, shelters, and first aid kits at the ready. Despite the challenges, people found ways to cope and even thrive, with community spirit and resilience playing a key role in getting through the difficult times.

  • Rationing of food, fuel, and other essential items was a fact of life, with people having to be creative with limited resources
  • Blackouts were enforced to prevent German bombers from navigating, with windows and doors covered in blackout curtains or blinds
  • Air raid drills and evacuations were a regular occurrence, with people having to be prepared to seek shelter at a moment's notice

Life in the Anderson Shelter

Life in the Anderson shelter was cramped and uncomfortable, with families often having to huddle together in the small space. The shelter was designed to provide protection from bomb blasts, but it was not designed for comfort, and people often had to endure long periods of time in the shelter, waiting for the all-clear signal.

Despite the difficulties, people made the best of the situation, with games, stories, and singing helping to pass the time. The shelter also became a symbol of community, with neighbours often sharing shelters and supporting each other through the difficult times.

Supporting the War Effort

People on the home front played a crucial role in supporting the war effort, with many contributing to the production of war materials, volunteering for civil defence, and participating in fundraising campaigns. Women, in particular, took on new roles, working in factories, on farms, and in other industries, helping to keep the country running.

  • Volunteering for civil defence, including roles such as air raid wardens, firefighters, and first aid responders
  • Participating in fundraising campaigns, such as the sale of war bonds and savings stamps
  • Contributing to the production of war materials, including working in factories and on farms
