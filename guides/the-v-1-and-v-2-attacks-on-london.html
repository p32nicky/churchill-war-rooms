The V-1 and V-2 Attacks on London

The 'doodlebugs' and rockets of the war's final phase.

The final phase of World War II saw the introduction of two new and terrifying weapons: the V-1 flying bomb and the V-2 rocket. These attacks on London had a profound impact on the city and its inhabitants, and are a key part of the story told at the Churchill War Rooms. In this article, we will explore the history of the V-1 and V-2 attacks, and their significance in the context of the war.

Introduction to the V-1 and V-2

The V-1 and V-2 were two types of weapons developed by Nazi Germany in the final years of the war. The V-1, also known as the "doodlebug" or "buzz bomb", was a flying bomb powered by a pulsejet engine, while the V-2 was a long-range ballistic missile. Both weapons were designed to be used against civilian targets, and were intended to break the morale of the British people.

The V-1 Attacks

The first V-1 attack on London took place in June 1944, just after the D-Day landings in Normandy. The V-1s were launched from sites in northern France, and flew towards London on a predetermined course. As they flew, the V-1s made a distinctive buzzing noise, which stopped just before the bomb exploded. This warning gave people a short time to seek shelter, but the V-1s were still responsible for significant damage and loss of life.

  • Over 2,500 V-1s were launched at London during the summer of 1944
  • The V-1s killed over 6,000 people and injured many more
  • The V-1 attacks caused significant damage to buildings and infrastructure

The V-2 Attacks

The V-2 rocket was a more sophisticated and deadly weapon than the V-1. It was powered by a liquid-fuelled rocket engine, and was capable of reaching speeds of over 3,000 miles per hour. The V-2s were launched from sites in the Netherlands, and were almost impossible to defend against. The first V-2 attack on London took place in September 1944, and the rockets continued to fall on the city until March 1945.

  • Over 1,100 V-2s were launched at London during the final months of the war
  • The V-2s killed over 2,700 people and injured many more
  • The V-2s caused significant damage to buildings and infrastructure, including the destruction of several hospitals and schools

Defending Against the V-1 and V-2

Defending against the V-1 and V-2 attacks was a significant challenge for the British military. The V-1s could be shot down by anti-aircraft guns, but the V-2s were too fast and too high to be intercepted. The British government also implemented a number of measures to reduce the impact of the attacks, including evacuating children and other vulnerable people from the city. A guided Westminster WWII walking tour can provide valuable context to the measures taken to defend the city and the impact of the attacks on its inhabitants.

Legacy of the V-1 and V-2 Attacks

The V-1 and V-2 attacks on London had a profound impact on the city and its people. The attacks caused significant damage and loss of life, and had a major impact on the morale of the British people. However, the attacks also brought people together, and helped to galvanise the British war effort. Today, the V-1 and V-2 attacks are remembered as an important part of the history of World War II, and are commemorated at the Churchill War Rooms and other museums and memorials in London.
