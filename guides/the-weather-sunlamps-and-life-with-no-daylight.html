The Weather, Sunlamps and Life With No Daylight

How staff coped with living and working below ground.

The Churchill War Rooms, hidden beneath the streets of London, served as the secret underground headquarters of the British government during World War II. For those who worked and lived here, life was far from ordinary, with the lack of natural daylight being just one of the many challenges they faced.

Artificial Sunlight

Staff at the War Rooms had to contend with the absence of natural daylight, which could take a toll on both their physical and mental health. To mitigate this, sunlamps were used to provide a substitute for natural sunlight. These lamps emitted ultraviolet rays, helping to prevent conditions such as rickets and other diseases caused by vitamin D deficiency. The use of sunlamps was a common practice in underground environments during the war, and the War Rooms were no exception.

Coping Mechanisms

Despite the difficulties of working in a subterranean environment, staff at the War Rooms developed various coping mechanisms to deal with the lack of daylight. Some of these included taking regular breaks in the outdoor areas above ground, when possible, and engaging in physical activities to stay healthy and active. The proximity of the War Rooms to other government buildings and landmarks, such as the Houses of Parliament, also allowed staff to take short walks outside during their breaks, providing a much-needed respite from the underground tunnels.

Working Conditions

The working conditions at the War Rooms were often cramped and claustrophobic, with staff working long hours in a windowless environment. To alleviate this, the rooms were equipped with ventilation systems and air conditioning to maintain a comfortable temperature and air quality. Additionally, the War Rooms were designed to be self-sufficient, with their own facilities, including a hospital, kitchen, and dormitories, to support the staff who worked and lived on site.

  • Map rooms for tracking military operations
  • Conference rooms for strategic planning
  • Telephone exchange and teleprinter rooms for communication

Living Quarters

For those who lived on site, the War Rooms provided basic but functional living quarters. The dormitories were equipped with bunk beds, washbasins, and toilets, and staff had access to a kitchen and dining area. While the living conditions were Spartan, they were designed to support the staff who worked long hours, often for extended periods, without a break. The close proximity of the living quarters to the working areas also helped to reduce the need for lengthy commutes, allowing staff to rest and recover quickly.

Adding Context to Your Visit

A visit to the Churchill War Rooms can be a powerful and moving experience, providing a unique insight into the lives of those who worked and lived beneath the streets of London during World War II.
