V1 and V2 Rockets: The Attacks on London

Hitler's 'vengeance weapons' and their impact on wartime London.

When the Luftwaffe turned to the so‑called “vengeance weapons” in the latter stages of the conflict, the city that had endured the Blitz now faced a new, terrifying kind of assault. The V1 flying bomb and the V2 rocket rained destruction on London, reshaping civilian life, prompting rapid defensive innovations and leaving a legacy that still haunts the capital’s streets.

V1 “Buzz Bombs”: Germany’s First Cruise Missile

The V1, nicknamed the “buzz bomb” because of its distinctive pulse‑jet engine, was the world’s first operational cruise missile. Deployed from concealed launch ramps in occupied northern France and the Netherlands, each weapon carried a warhead of roughly one‑tonne explosive. When they began striking London in the summer of 1944, they arrived silently, then emitted a brief, eerie drone before crashing into residential districts.

How the V1 was launched

  • Fixed concrete ramps angled toward the English coast.
  • Mobile launch platforms that could be quickly assembled and hidden.
  • Occasionally, aircraft dropped the missile over the Channel for a final glide.

The impact was swift and brutal. Whole streets were reduced to rubble, and the unpredictable nature of the attacks meant that ordinary citizens never knew when the next “buzz” would signal a bomb about to hit. Casualties ran into the thousands over a few months, and the psychological strain on Londoners was profound, as the city’s night‑time calm was shattered by a new, relentless threat.

V2 “Vergeltungswaffe 2”: The First Ballistic Missile

Following the V1, the Germans introduced the V2 – a liquid‑fuelled ballistic missile that travelled faster than the speed of sound. Launched from fortified sites in the same occupied territories, the V2 followed a high‑arc trajectory, disappearing from sight before re‑entering the atmosphere and striking without warning. Because it gave no audible cue, the V2’s impact was felt only as a sudden explosion and a plume of fire.

Why the V2 was terrifying

  • Supersonic speed made it impossible to hear before impact.
  • High‑altitude launch meant radar could not reliably track it.
  • Each missile carried a larger warhead, causing more extensive damage.

Although fewer V2s fell on London than V1s, each strike left a deep scar. The suddenness of the attacks amplified fear, and the knowledge that the weapon was technically a precursor to modern rockets added a chilling sense of inevitability to the war’s technological escalation.

London’s Defences and Civilian Response

The city’s authorities responded with a mixture of old‑fashioned ingenuity and emerging technology. Anti‑aircraft guns, barrage balloons and sound‑locators were repositioned to cover likely V1 flight paths, while the Royal Observer Corps kept watch for the faint drone of an incoming bomb. On the ground, the network of public shelters – from underground stations to purpose‑built Anderson shelters – was expanded, and volunteers formed firewatch teams to tackle incendiary fires before they could spread.

  • “Diver” alerts warned residents to seek shelter when a V1 was sighted.
  • Mobile “rocket‑catcher” units attempted to destroy V1s mid‑flight using anti‑aircraft fire.
  • Neighbourhood “Air Raid Precaution” (ARP) wardens coordinated evacuations and first‑aid.

These measures, combined with the resilience of Londoners who kept shops open and children at school where possible, helped to blunt the overall impact of the rocket campaign.

Strategic Consequences for the War Effort

While the V‑weapons inflicted civilian casualties and terror, they strained German resources more than they crippled British morale. The production of V1s and V2s demanded vast quantities of fuel, specialised engineering talent and secure launch sites, all of which were increasingly vulnerable to Allied air raids. In turn, the Allies mounted intensive bombing raids on the launch ramps and supply depots, gradually curtailing the frequency of attacks.

Intelligence breakthroughs – notably the deciphering of German communications and the work of resistance networks in occupied Europe – also played a crucial role. By the end of the campaign, the flow of rockets into London had dwindled, and the German focus shifted back to defending the Reich against the advancing Allied armies.

Remembering the Rockets Today

Visitors to the Churchill War Rooms can explore a dedicated exhibition that recreates the atmosphere of the rocket attacks, complete with original propaganda, recovered fragments and interactive maps of launch sites. The display places the V‑weapons within the broader narrative of London’s wartime endurance, illustrating how the city adapted to ever‑changing threats.

For those who wish to see the physical traces of the campaign, a guided Westminster WWII walking tour offers a contextual glimpse of the areas most affected, linking the story of the rockets to the streets, churches and memorials that still bear their scars.
