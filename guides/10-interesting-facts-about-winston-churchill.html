10 Interesting Facts About Winston Churchill

Surprising and little-known facts about Britain's wartime leader.

Winston Churchill is instantly recognisable as the indomitable British leader who steered the nation through the darkest days of the Second World War. Yet beyond the iconic speeches and the familiar portrait, his life is peppered with surprising episodes that reveal a man of contradictions, daring, and unexpected talents. Below are ten lesser‑known facts that help to round out the picture of Britain’s wartime lion.

1. A youthful soldier with a taste for adventure

Before he ever entered Parliament, Churchill carved out a colourful military résumé that reads like a 19th‑century adventure novel.

  • He served as a cavalry officer in the British Army and took part in the Battle of Omdurman (Sudan, 1898), where he famously rode his horse, Julius Caesar, into the fray.
  • His next posting saw him as a war correspondent on the front lines of the Boer War, where he was captured and made a daring escape from a Boer prison camp in Bloemfontein.
  • These early exploits earned him the nickname “the Lion of the Desert” long before the 1940s, and they also gave him a lifelong fascination with the art of warfare.

2. A prolific writer who won the Nobel Prize for Literature

Churchill’s pen was as sharp as his rhetoric on the battlefield. Over a six‑decade career he authored more than 40 books, ranging from detailed histories of the World Wars to vivid travelogues of his many journeys.

  • His multi‑volume work The Second World War remains a cornerstone of modern military historiography, praised for its blend of personal insight and exhaustive research.
  • In 1953, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature not for a single masterpiece but for his “mastery of historical and biographical description” and his “brilliant oratory” that “has added to the literature of the age”.
  • He continued to write well into his nineties, completing A History of the English-Speaking Peoples just months before his death.

3. The “painted” prime minister

When the bombs fell over London, many expected the prime minister to retreat to a safe, undisclosed bunker. Instead, Churchill insisted on staying at the underground complex that would later become the Churchill War Rooms.

  • He personally oversaw the installation of a custom‑made, sound‑proofed “map room” where he could plot enemy movements without the risk of eavesdropping.
  • His habit of working in a dimly lit room, surrounded by cigars and a glass of whisky, earned him the affectionate moniker “the bulldog in a smoking jacket”.
  • These very rooms are now open to the public, offering a tangible glimpse of the wartime command centre.

4. A lover of the arts and a talented amateur painter

Beyond politics, Churchill found solace on canvas. He began painting in his forties, using it as a therapeutic escape from the pressures of public life.

  • He favoured landscapes and seascapes, often working en plein air in the French Riviera, the Scottish Highlands, and the coast of Malta.
  • His works were exhibited at the Royal Academy and fetched respectable sums at auction, though he never pursued a commercial career.
  • He once joked that “painting is a wonderful way of forgetting the world and its troubles for a while”, a sentiment that resonated with many who saw his art as a window into his quieter side.

5. A complex relationship with the press

Churchill’s early career was marked by a fierce rivalry with the newspaper magnates of his day, yet he also understood the power of media to shape public opinion.

  • He wrote for the Morning Post and later for the Evening Standard, honing a style that blended vivid description with political analysis.
  • During the war he cultivated a close bond with the BBC, delivering radio speeches that became legendary for their resolve and rhythm.
  • His famous “We shall fight on the beaches” address was broadcast live, reaching millions and cementing his status as a master of wartime communication.

6. Personal quirks that baffled colleagues

Even the most disciplined leader has idiosyncrasies, and Churchill was no exception.

  • He was notorious for his prodigious appetite, famously claiming that a “proper breakfast” should consist of bacon, eggs, toast, and a glass of orange juice – a ritual he never missed, even during the Blitz.
  • He kept a daily schedule that included a “five‑minute nap” after lunch, a habit he believed sharpened his mental acuity.
  • His love of cigars was legendary; he once smoked a cigar while delivering a speech, later quipping that the smoke “kept the enemy’s bullets at bay”.

7. A statesman with a global outlook

Churchill’s vision extended far beyond Britain’s shores.

  • He was a vocal advocate for a united Europe after the war, warning that “the continent must not be left to the whims of a single power”.
  • His relationship with the United States deepened after his 1941 Atlantic Charter meeting with President Roosevelt, laying foundations for the “special relationship” that persists today.
  • He championed the formation of the United Nations, insisting that “peace can only be kept by a world organisation that binds together the great powers”.

8. A family man with a turbulent private life

Behind the public persona lay a complicated domestic world.

  • He married Clementine Hozier in 1908; their partnership endured the strains of war, politics, and personal loss, and she remained his confidante until his death.
  • His relationship with his son, Randolph, was strained, partly due to Randolph’s own political ambitions and the shadow cast by his father’s legacy.
  • Churchill’s diaries reveal a man who, despite his stoic image, often expressed deep anxieties about his health and the future of his country.

9. A post‑war advocate for scientific progress

After 1945, Churchill turned his attention to the emerging scientific frontier.

  • He delivered the 1946 “Sinews of Peace” speech in Fulton, Missouri, famously coining the term “Iron Curtain” and highlighting the need for Western scientific cooperation.
  • He supported the development of nuclear energy for peaceful purposes, urging the United Kingdom to invest in research that could “light the world without destroying it”.
  • His patronage helped establish the UK’s first atomic energy research programme, a legacy that still influences Britain’s energy policy.

10. Legacy that lives on in the cityscape

Beyond statues and streets named after him, Churchill’s imprint on London can be explored on foot.

A guided Westminster WWII walking tour is a great way to add context to many of these anecdotes, taking visitors from the Houses of Parliament to the secret tunnels beneath the city where Churchill once plotted his strategies.

From the preserved war rooms beneath Whitehall to the modest cottage where he painted in his later years, the capital offers countless touchpoints that keep the memory of Britain’s wartime lion alive for future generations.
