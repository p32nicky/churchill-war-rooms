St Paul's Survives the Blitz

The famous image of the cathedral standing amid the fire.

The iconic image of St Paul's Cathedral standing tall amidst the flames and smoke of the Blitz is one of the most enduring symbols of London's resilience during World War II. This powerful image, captured by photographer Herbert Mason, has become synonymous with the city's ability to withstand even the most intense bombardment.

Introduction to the Blitz

The Blitz, which lasted from September 1940 to May 1941, was a period of sustained bombing of London by the German Air Force, known as the Luftwaffe. The campaign was designed to weaken the British people's resolve and prepare the way for a potential German invasion. However, the Blitz had the opposite effect, with Londoners rallying together to support each other and defend their city.

The Night of December 29, 1940

On the night of December 29, 1940, the Luftwaffe launched a devastating attack on the City of London, with wave after wave of bombers dropping incendiary bombs on the area. The resulting fires raged out of control, threatening to engulf St Paul's Cathedral, which stood at the heart of the city. However, thanks to the bravery and quick thinking of the cathedral's firewatchers, the building was saved, and the iconic image of St Paul's standing amidst the flames was born.

Defending St Paul's

The defence of St Paul's Cathedral during the Blitz was a major priority for the British authorities. A team of firewatchers, led by the cathedral's Surveyor to the Fabric, Godfrey Allen, worked tirelessly to protect the building from the falling bombs and incendiary devices. Their efforts were aided by the use of:

  • Firefighting equipment, including hoses and pumps
  • Sandbags, which were used to smother incendiary bombs
  • Steeplejacks, who climbed the cathedral's dome to remove unexploded bombs
These efforts ensured that St Paul's remained standing, even as the surrounding area was reduced to rubble.

Rebuilding and Restoration

Although St Paul's Cathedral survived the Blitz, it was not entirely unscathed. The building suffered significant damage, including broken windows, damaged stonework, and a cracked dome. In the years following the war, a major programme of rebuilding and restoration was undertaken to repair the damage and restore the cathedral to its former glory. This work included the replacement of the cathedral's windows, the repair of the stonework, and the strengthening of the dome.

Visiting St Paul's Today

Today, visitors to St Paul's Cathedral can still see evidence of the building's wartime history, including the scars of the Blitz and the memorial to the firewatchers who defended the cathedral. A guided Westminster WWII walking tour is a great way to add context to a visit to St Paul's, providing a deeper understanding of the cathedral's role in the war and the experiences of those who lived and worked in the area during this time. The cathedral remains an important symbol of London's resilience and determination, and its iconic dome continues to dominate the city's skyline.
