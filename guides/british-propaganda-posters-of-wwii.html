British Propaganda Posters of WWII

'Keep Calm', 'Dig for Victory' and Britain's wartime poster art.

The visual language of Britain’s wartime propaganda is instantly recognisable: bold type, bright colours, and a steady stream of slogans that urged citizens to stay calm, work hard, and support the war effort. From the iconic “Keep Calm and Carry On” to the ubiquitous “Dig for Victory” campaign, these posters not only shaped public behaviour but also left a lasting imprint on the nation’s cultural memory.

The “Keep Calm” Phenomenon

Originally designed in 1939 by the Ministry of Information, the “Keep Calm and Carry On” poster was intended for distribution after the first major air raids. Its simple layout—white lettering on a red background—conveyed a stoic reassurance that the government hoped would steadily guide a frightened public. Although it was not widely displayed during the war, the design was later rediscovered in the early 2000s and has become a global design staple.

  • Typeface: a modified Gill Sans, chosen for its clarity.
  • Colour scheme: red for urgency, white for visibility.
  • Message tone: calm authority, avoiding alarmist language.

Dig for Victory: Mobilising the Home Front

When food imports were threatened by German U‑boats, the government turned the countryside and city backyards into productive plots. The “Dig for Victory” series featured cheerful illustrations of families with spades, encouraging everyone—from schoolchildren to shopkeepers—to grow their own vegetables.

  • Key visual: a smiling farmer holding a basket of produce.
  • Slogans varied: “Grow it yourself”, “Turn your garden into a kitchen”, “Every plot counts”.
  • Impact: By 1943, home‑grown produce supplied a significant proportion of the nation’s fresh vegetables.

Designers, Agencies, and the Machinery of Persuasion

The Ministry of Information recruited leading commercial art firms, such as the renowned Lintas and the wartime branch of the London Press Exchange. Graphic artists like Abram Games, known as “the father of modern British poster art”, introduced bold geometric forms and striking colour contrasts that made messages instantly legible from a distance.

Notable contributors

  • Abram Games – responsible for the famous “Your Britain—Fight for it” series.
  • James Boswell – created the “Your Country Needs You” recruitment posters.
  • John Minton – supplied the stylised silhouettes used in civil defence appeals.

These agencies operated under tight deadlines, often producing a new poster every few days to respond to shifting wartime priorities, from air‑raid precautions to rationing reminders.

Messages of Unity, Morale, and Duty

Beyond practical instructions, many posters aimed to forge a collective identity. The recurring motif of the “Britain United” figure—a stylised, sturdy silhouette holding a torch—appeared on leaflets, rail posters, and even on the sides of wartime trucks. The visual language reinforced the idea that every citizen, regardless of class or region, shared a common purpose.

  • Air‑raid warnings: bold red triangles with the phrase “Keep Clear of the Doorways”.
  • Rationing appeals: illustrated pantry shelves with the tagline “Make Every Bite Count”.
  • Women’s workforce recruitment: “Women of Britain, Join the Ranks” featuring a female factory worker.

Legacy and Where to See Them Today

Original wartime posters survive in the collections of the Imperial War Museum, the National Archives, and several regional museums. Reproductions are frequently displayed in heritage sites such as the Churchill War Rooms, where visitors can view them in the context of the very rooms where policy decisions were made.

For those wishing to place these images within the city’s wartime geography, a guided Westminster WWII walking tour offers an excellent opportunity to see surviving signage, bomb‑damage remnants, and the very streets that once echoed with the slogans described above. The tour provides context that deepens appreciation of how visual propaganda intersected with everyday life.

Modern designers continue to reference these posters, whether in fashion, graphic design curricula, or public art projects that reinterpret the original messages for contemporary audiences. Their enduring appeal lies in the combination of clear visual hierarchy, concise wording, and an emotional resonance that still feels relevant when societies face collective challenges.
