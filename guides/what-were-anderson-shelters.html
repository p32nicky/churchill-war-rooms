What Were Anderson Shelters?

The backyard air-raid shelters that protected wartime Britain.

When the Blitz turned the night sky over Britain into a sea of searchlights and flak, the government looked for a quick, cheap way to keep civilians safe without turning every street into a concrete bunker. The answer was the Anderson shelter – a corrugated‑iron, half‑submerged hut that could be bolted into a typical garden. Over the course of the war, millions of families slipped these modest structures under their lawns, creating a network of private air‑raid shelters that saved countless lives.

Origins and Design

Who was Sir John Anderson?

The shelters bear the name of the Home Secretary who championed them, Sir John Anderson. In 1939 he authorised a programme to mass‑produce a simple, prefabricated shelter that could be assembled by ordinary people with minimal tools.

  • Corrugated iron panels – lightweight yet strong enough to resist blast pressure.
  • Curved roof – distributes force and sheds rain.
  • Four‑inch thick concrete slab – forms the base, buried half‑deep in the garden.
  • Standard dimensions – roughly 6 ft long, 4 ft wide, and 6 ft high at the ridge.

How They Were Built

Materials and Construction

The government supplied the metal kit and a set of instructions; families sourced the concrete base locally. Installation could be completed in a weekend, even by those with no building experience.

  • Excavate a shallow trench, usually 2‑3 ft deep.
  • Lay the concrete slab, allowing it to set.
  • Position the prefabricated iron frame over the slab and bolt it down.
  • Cover the roof with a waterproof canvas or tarpaulin for extra protection.

Because the shelters were designed to be partially underground, they blended into the garden landscape, leaving the surface free for everyday use.

Everyday Life Inside

Living Conditions

For most families an Anderson shelter became a second living room during raids. The space was cramped, but it offered a dry, relatively comfortable refuge compared with the cold streets.

  • Seating – often a wooden bench or a pile of blankets.
  • Lighting – a battery‑powered torch or a small oil lamp.
  • Ventilation – a simple vent pipe prevented suffocation.
  • Supplies – families kept a basic emergency kit: bottled water, a tin of biscuits, a first‑aid box, and a radio for updates.

Children were encouraged to bring a favourite toy, and many households stored a small radio to hear the BBC’s wartime broadcasts, turning the shelter into a hub of information as well as safety.

Distribution and Numbers

Government Rollout

The Ministry of Home Security coordinated a nationwide campaign, advertising the shelters in newspapers, on posters, and via radio. Local councils organised distribution points where families could collect their kits.

  • Affordability – the cost was heavily subsidised; many families paid a modest fee or received the shelter free of charge.
  • Coverage – shelters were issued to urban, suburban and even many rural households, reflecting the belief that any area could be targeted.
  • Scale – by the height of the Blitz, the programme had supplied shelters to a substantial proportion of private homes across the United Kingdom.

The widespread adoption meant that, even when the Luftwaffe shifted tactics, a large portion of the civilian population could take cover without having to queue at public shelters.

Legacy and Preservation

Surviving Examples

Today a handful of Anderson shelters survive in gardens, museums and heritage sites. They serve as tangible reminders of the home front’s resilience and ingenuity. Visiting a preserved shelter offers a visceral sense of the cramped conditions and the community spirit that defined the era.

For those keen to place the shelters in a broader wartime context, a guided Westminster WWII walking tour is a great way to add depth, linking the humble garden huts to the strategic decisions made in the very streets you are walking.

Whether you encounter an original shelter in a London back garden or see a replica in a museum, the Anderson shelter remains a symbol of ordinary citizens taking extraordinary measures to protect themselves and their neighbours during one of Britain’s darkest hours.
