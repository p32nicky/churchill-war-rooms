What Were Barrage Balloons?

The giant balloons that floated over wartime London's skies.

When you look up at photographs of wartime London, the sky is often dotted with huge, white spheres tethered to the ground – the iconic barrage balloons that floated above the city’s streets, parks and riverbanks during the Second World War.

What Were Barrage Balloons?

Barrage balloons were large, helium‑filled aerostats deliberately anchored to the ground with steel cables. Their primary role was defensive: to create a physical barrier that could damage or deter low‑flying enemy aircraft attempting to conduct strafing runs or drop bombs. Though they looked simple, each balloon was part of a sophisticated system of aerial protection that complemented anti‑aircraft guns and night‑fighter patrols.

Purpose and Design

Deterrent and Defence

The presence of a network of cables forced hostile pilots to fly at higher altitudes, where bomb‑dropping accuracy fell and aircraft became more vulnerable to ground‑based fire. In addition, a direct collision with a cable could tear a wing or cause a catastrophic loss of control, making the balloons a lethal obstacle as well as a visual warning.

  • Typical balloons measured around 30‑40 metres in diameter.
  • Helium provided lift; a small amount of hydrogen was occasionally used when supplies ran low.
  • Steel cables ranged from 1 inch to 2 inches in diameter, extending several metres below the balloon.
  • Each balloon could stay aloft for several days before requiring deflation and replacement.

How They Worked

Tethering and Cables

Every balloon was anchored by a winch‑driven cable that could be raised or lowered according to weather conditions and operational needs. When a raid was imminent, the cables were hoisted to their full length, creating a dense web over strategic zones. In calm weather, the balloons could be lowered to the ground to protect them from high winds, which could otherwise damage the envelope or snap the cables.

  • Winches were operated by Royal Engineers or local Air Ministry personnel.
  • Safety valves released excess gas to prevent over‑inflation.
  • Ground crews regularly inspected cables for corrosion and fatigue.
  • Night‑time illumination was provided by low‑intensity lamps to avoid silhouetting the balloons.

Deployment Over London

Key Sites

From the early days of the Blitz, the Ministry of Defence mapped out a grid of “balloon belts” that encircled the city’s most vulnerable targets – the docks, the financial district, railway stations and industrial factories. The balloons were not static; they were moved and repositioned as the tactical situation evolved.

  • Westminster and the Houses of Parliament were protected by a dense cluster of balloons.
  • The Thames River corridor featured a line of balloons to shield bridges and warehouses.
  • Royal Parks, such as Hyde Park and Regent’s Park, hosted temporary balloon stations to cover open green spaces.
  • Outer suburbs, including Woolwich and Battersea, held secondary belts to catch aircraft that slipped past the inner ring.

Life Around the Balloons

Safety Measures

For civilians, the balloons became a familiar part of the cityscape. Their presence imposed restrictions: children were warned to stay clear of the cables, and motorists were advised to avoid low‑bridge passages where the balloons hung low. The cables were marked with orange flags and occasionally illuminated with small lanterns to warn night‑time traffic.

  • Local authorities posted signage indicating “No‑Fly” zones beneath the balloons.
  • Police and fire brigades received training on how to cut cables in emergencies.
  • Residents were encouraged to report any damage to the balloons or cables promptly.
  • Special “balloon patrols” inspected the sites each morning and evening.

Legacy and Remembrance

Today, only a handful of original barrage balloons survive in museums, but their legacy lives on in the stories told by the Churchill War Rooms and other historic sites. Walking the streets where the balloons once hovered offers a tangible connection to the city’s wartime resilience. For those seeking a deeper context, a guided Westminster WWII walking tour can weave the story of the balloons into the broader narrative of London’s defence during the Blitz.

  • Interpretive plaques at former balloon sites explain their strategic importance.
  • Annual commemorations often include reenactments of balloon deployment.
  • Archival photographs and maps are displayed at the Imperial War Museum.
  • Modern aerial‑defence systems trace their conceptual roots back to the barrage‑balloon network.
