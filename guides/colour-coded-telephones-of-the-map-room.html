Colour-Coded Telephones of the Map Room

The famous 'beauty chorus' of coloured phones.

The Churchill War Rooms, hidden beneath the streets of Whitehall, are a fascinating piece of British history, offering a glimpse into the secret underground headquarters that played a crucial role in the Allied victory during World War II. One of the most intriguing features of the War Rooms is the Map Room, where a 'beauty chorus' of colour-coded telephones stood ready to connect the British government with its allies and military leaders around the world.

Introduction to the Map Room

The Map Room was the nerve centre of the War Rooms, where a team of cartographers, intelligence officers, and military strategists worked tirelessly to track the progress of the war. The room was dominated by large maps of Europe, Africa, and Asia, which were updated constantly to reflect the changing situation on the ground. It was here that the famous colour-coded telephones were located, each one linked to a different theatre of operation or government department.

The Colour-Coded Telephones

The colour-coded telephones were a key feature of the Map Room, allowing staff to quickly identify the source of an incoming call and respond accordingly. The colours used were carefully chosen to be easily distinguishable, even in the low-light conditions of the underground bunker. The main colours used were red, green, yellow, and blue, each with its own specific meaning.

  • Red phones were used for direct communication with the Prime Minister and other senior government officials
  • Green phones connected the Map Room to the military commanders in the field
  • Yellow phones linked the War Rooms to the intelligence services and code-breakers at Bletchley Park
  • Blue phones were used for communication with the Royal Navy and other Allied navies

How the Telephones Worked

The colour-coded telephones were connected to a complex network of switchboards and exchanges, which allowed calls to be quickly routed to the correct destination. The system was designed to be highly flexible, with multiple lines and operators available to handle a high volume of calls. The phones themselves were specially designed to be durable and reliable, with features such as secure scramblers to protect sensitive information.

Life in the Map Room

Working in the Map Room was a demanding and intense experience, with staff often working long hours in cramped and noisy conditions. Despite the challenges, the team in the Map Room played a vital role in the Allied war effort, providing critical support to military commanders and government officials. Visitors to the Churchill War Rooms can still see the original Map Room, complete with its colour-coded telephones, to get a glimpse of what life was like during this pivotal period in history.

Adding Context to Your Visit

A visit to the Churchill War Rooms can be even more meaningful when combined with a guided Westminster WWII walking tour, which provides a broader context for the events and decisions that took place in the War Rooms. By exploring the streets and buildings of Westminster, visitors can gain a deeper understanding of the role that London played in the war effort, and how the city was affected by the conflict.

Preserving History for the Future

Today, the Churchill War Rooms are a museum, preserved to show future generations the importance of the role that this underground bunker played in shaping the course of history. The colour-coded telephones of the Map Room remain one of the most popular and enduring exhibits, a testament to the ingenuity and determination of the people who worked there during World War II. By visiting the War Rooms, visitors can pay their respects to the men and women who served in this critical facility, and gain a new appreciation for the significance of this pivotal moment in British history.
