El Alamein: The Turning of the Tide

The victory Churchill called the 'end of the beginning'.

The Churchill War Rooms, a museum showcasing Winston Churchill's leadership during World War II, highlights several pivotal battles that led to the Allied victory. One such battle, El Alamein, marked a significant turning point in the war and is often regarded as the 'end of the beginning' by Churchill himself.

Introduction to El Alamein

El Alamein, a small Egyptian town on the Mediterranean coast, was the site of a decisive battle fought between the Allied forces, primarily comprising British, Australian, and New Zealand troops, and the Axis powers, consisting of German and Italian forces. The battle took place between October and November 1942, and its outcome had far-reaching consequences for the war in North Africa.

The Strategic Importance of El Alamein

The town of El Alamein was strategically located, controlling the access to the Nile River and the Suez Canal, a vital waterway connecting the Mediterranean to the Red Sea. The Axis powers, led by Field Marshal Erwin Rommel, aimed to capture the Suez Canal and gain control over the region, while the Allies sought to prevent this and ultimately drive the Axis forces out of Egypt.

The Battle of El Alamein

The Battle of El Alamein was a complex and intense conflict, involving large-scale artillery bombardments, infantry assaults, and tank battles. The Allies, under the command of General Bernard Montgomery, had been preparing for the battle, accumulating significant resources and manpower. The Axis forces, on the other hand, were overstretched and struggling with supply chain issues. Key events of the battle include:

  • The initial Allied artillery bombardment, which caught the Axis forces off guard
  • The fierce fighting at Kidney Ridge, where the Allies gained a crucial foothold
  • The eventual breakout of the Allied forces, leading to the collapse of the Axis lines

Aftermath and Significance

The victory at El Alamein marked a significant shift in the balance of power in North Africa, as the Axis forces began to retreat. The battle also had a profound impact on the morale of the Allied troops, demonstrating that they could defeat the previously invincible Rommel and his forces. Churchill's description of the battle as the 'end of the beginning' acknowledges the significance of El Alamein as a turning point in the war, after which the Allies would gradually gain the upper hand.
